PRESERVATION

A record preserved so that it can be examined without relying upon memory alone.

PRESERVE FIRST. EXAMINE SECOND. CONCLUDE LAST.

1. Why Preservation Matters

The purpose of preservation is simple: to prevent the documentary record from disappearing, being silently altered, becoming detached from its source, or being reduced to an unsupported summary of what someone remembers.

The archive therefore treats preservation as a discipline in its own right. The underlying record comes before the interpretation of the record.

PRESERVATION IS NOT PROOF
PRESERVATION MAKES EXAMINATION POSSIBLE

2. What Is Being Preserved

Primary Documents

Government correspondence, official records, legal and administrative material, reports, statements and other source documents.

Communications

Messages, emails, correspondence, recordings, transcripts, call records and related documentary material.

Writings

Books, essays, statements, research, testimony, spiritual writing, prophecy, poetry and other authored material.

Digital Records

Files, filenames, paths, repositories, commits, hashes, timestamps and other available provenance information.

3. The Preservation Chain

Where the necessary information exists, the archive seeks to retain a documentary chain from the originating material to the public preservation copy.

ORIGINAL SOURCE → FILE → HASH → TIMESTAMP → REPOSITORY → COMMIT → PUBLIC ARCHIVE

Not every record contains every element of this chain. Missing provenance is identified rather than invented.

4. SHA-256 and Digital Integrity

A SHA-256 hash can provide a digital fingerprint of a file. If the file changes, its hash will normally change. This can therefore assist with demonstrating that a particular digital object has remained unchanged since a particular hashing event.

Important distinction: a hash can help establish digital identity or integrity. It does not establish that the contents of a document are true, that an allegation occurred, that a person is responsible, or that a legal conclusion follows.

5. Blockchain and Timestamp Records

Where records have been anchored through blockchain or timestamp systems, those mechanisms may provide an additional layer of preservation and provenance.

The archive does not treat blockchain anchoring as a substitute for examination of the underlying document.

What It May Support

Existence of a digital object at or before a recorded point in time, depending on the system and evidence available.

What It Does Not Prove

The substantive truth of every statement contained within the preserved object.

6. Provenance

Provenance asks where a document came from and how it reached the archive. Where available, the archive seeks to preserve information such as:

Source

Originating organisation, person, system or repository.

Date

Creation, transmission, publication or other relevant date.

Identity

Title, filename, document number or other identifying information.

Location

Original URL, repository path or other source location where available.

Integrity

Hash, commit, timestamp or other technical record where available.

Relationship

Connections to related documents, events, correspondence or testimony.

8. Preservation Copy and Reconstruction

Where an archive has been reconstructed from a preservation copy, the reconstruction should be understood as a finding aid rather than as a replacement for the preserved source material.

The objective is to retain the documentary relationship between a record and its provenance, while making a large body of material easier for journalists, lawyers, researchers, historians, advocates and other readers to examine.

Underlying records should not be altered merely to make the story cleaner. If a filename, date, attribution, classification or provenance is uncertain, the uncertainty should remain visible.

9. Preservation Rules

1 — Preserve

Keep the source record before attempting to explain it.

2 — Identify

Record what the object is, where it came from and what is known about it.

3 — Distinguish

Separate source material from testimony, allegation, inference and interpretation.

4 — Verify

Where verification is possible, test claims against independent records.

5 — Preserve Uncertainty

Do not turn an unresolved matter into a finding merely because it appears persuasive.

6 — Link Back

Where possible, give readers a path to the original public source.

10. Classification

The archive uses an A–E framework as a finding-aid discipline. It is not a legal evidentiary standard and does not determine the truth of a proposition.

A — Documented Fact

Supported directly by an authoritative or primary record, subject to its scope.

B — Corroborated Claim

A claim supported by more than one relevant source or independent record.

C — Documented Allegation

An allegation recorded in documentary material but not thereby established as fact.

D — Inference

An interpretation or conclusion drawn from available material.

E — Speculation

A possibility that lacks sufficient documentary support.

11. Preservation of Testimony

Personal testimony has a legitimate place within a documentary archive. It records what a person says they experienced, remembered, observed or understood.

But testimony and independently established fact are not identical categories. The archive therefore seeks to preserve testimony without disguising it as an official finding.

ACCOUNT → DATE → SOURCE → DOCUMENT → CORROBORATION → STATUS

12. Preservation of Faith Writing

The archive also preserves spiritual, Gospel and prophetic writing. These materials are part of the author's intellectual, creative and spiritual record.

Statements concerning God, calling, prophecy, divine purpose or spiritual meaning are presented as faith, testimony or interpretation unless independently established by an appropriate source.

“They could not erase what God preserved” is retained in this archive as a statement of faith and spiritual interpretation, not as an independently verified supernatural finding.

13. Preservation First

The central principle is deliberately simple:

PRESERVE FIRST.
EXAMINE SECOND.
CONCLUDE LAST.

A record does not become less important because its meaning remains unresolved. Sometimes the most responsible archival statement is simply: UNRESOLVED.
