Writing is not separate from the archive. It is one of the principal ways experience, research, ideas, testimony, faith and interpretation have been preserved.

This page provides a catalogue of major works identified within the archive. The works themselves should be distinguished from the documentary records used to examine claims made within them.

WRITING IS A SOURCE OF TESTIMONY — NOT AUTOMATICALLY PROOF OF ITS CONTENT.

A published work may document what its author believed, experienced, researched or argued. Where factual propositions require verification, readers should consult the underlying documentary record.

Writing as Preservation

Across the archive, writing serves several functions: autobiography, research, testimony, declaration, advocacy, creative expression, spiritual reflection and preservation.

A document can preserve what a person said. A body of writing can preserve how that person understood what happened.

Those are related but different forms of evidence. The archive therefore keeps the author's voice visible while maintaining distinctions between testimony, documentary fact, interpretation and faith.

Source Integrity

The archive's principle is simple: preserve the original writing and keep its provenance visible.

ORIGINAL SOURCE

Preserve the underlying document whenever available.

PROVENANCE

Record filename, location, repository and relevant identifiers where available.

VERSION

Distinguish original material from later edits, reconstructions or summaries.

CONTEXT

Read writings alongside the documentary record rather than treating isolated passages as complete evidence.

The Boundary

NOT A COURT. NOT A VERDICT.

The presence of a statement, allegation, interpretation or belief in a published work does not by itself establish it as fact.

The archive preserves the writing so that it can be read, contextualised, tested and, where appropriate, corroborated or disputed.

The writing remains. The records remain. The reader is invited to examine them.
