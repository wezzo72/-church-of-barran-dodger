Why a Timeline?

A timeline places documents, writings, experiences and public events into chronological context. It helps a reader move from isolated records toward a documented sequence.

PRESERVE FIRST. EXAMINE SECOND. CONCLUDE LAST.

This chronology is a finding aid. It is not presented as a definitive history of every event in the author's life, nor does inclusion of an event establish that every interpretation attached to it is true.

Dates and descriptions should therefore be read together with their underlying source material. Where a date, event or interpretation remains uncertain, that uncertainty should remain visible.

How to Read the Chronology

A date can establish when a document was created, published, received, filed or otherwise recorded. It does not automatically establish the truth of every statement contained within that document.

DOCUMENTED DATE A date supported by an identifiable documentary or official record.
AUTHOR ACCOUNT A date or event reported in the author's testimony or writing.
CORROBORATED An account supported by additional identifiable material.
DISPUTED / UNRESOLVED A matter for which competing accounts, uncertainty or insufficient documentary material remains.

A Note on Conclusions

The purpose of chronology is not to manufacture a conclusion from proximity of dates.

Events appearing near one another in time may be connected, unrelated, disputed or only apparently connected. The archive therefore separates chronology from interpretation and interpretation from conclusion.

NOT A COURT. NOT A VERDICT.

This archive does not determine criminal guilt, establish liability, replace legal advice or independently establish every claim made within the material. Readers should examine the underlying records and the status assigned to each proposition.
